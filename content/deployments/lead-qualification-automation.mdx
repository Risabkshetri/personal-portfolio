## The constraint

The client had more inbound than they could work, and no consistent way to tell a
serious lead from a tyre-kicker until someone had spent 20 minutes on a call.
Qualification criteria existed only in the founder's head, and shifted slightly
each week. Leads arrived across a web form, WhatsApp, and email, landed in
different places, and the first-response time on the best leads was measured in
days.

The hard part was not the model. It was reconstructing what "a good lead"
actually meant for this business from watching how deals had actually gone.

## What I built

A pipeline that runs on every inbound lead:

1. **Ingest & unify.** Form, WhatsApp, and email leads normalise into one record.
2. **Enrich.** Public signals about the company and contact are pulled in and
   summarised.
3. **Score & classify.** An LLM step maps the lead to the qualification criteria
   we reconstructed with the client, and attaches a short written rationale
   rather than just a number.
4. **Route.** High-scoring leads are pushed to the top of the sales team's list
   with the rationale attached; low-scoring ones get a lighter-touch path.

## What broke

- **The criteria were a moving target.** The first scoring rubric was obsolete
  within weeks because the client's focus shifted. The fix was making the rubric
  a config the client owns, not something baked into code.
- **Enrichment dead ends.** For a meaningful share of leads, there was simply no
  public signal. The step had to return "nothing found" cleanly instead of
  stalling the pipeline.
- **Over-automation risk.** Auto-demoting a lead the founder personally cared
  about erodes trust in the whole system fast. Low-confidence classifications
  now surface for a human rather than acting silently.
